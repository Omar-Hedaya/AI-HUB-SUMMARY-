📌 Key Laws & Principles Summary (Chapter 1)
• Energy Conservation (1st Law): $E_{\text{in}} - E_{\text{out}} = \Delta E$
• Zeroth Law: If $T_A = T_C$ and $T_B = T_C \implies T_A = T_B$ (Thermometry basis)
• Property Relations: $v = V/m$, $\rho = m/V = 1/v$, $h = H/m$, $u = U/m$
• Pressure Relations: $P_{\text{abs}} = P_{\text{atm}} + P_{\text{gage}}$  |  $P_{\text{vac}} = P_{\text{atm}} - P_{\text{abs}}$
• Thermodynamic Cycle: Initial state $\equiv$ Final state $\implies \Delta\text{Property} = 0$, $Q_{\text{net}} = W_{\text{net}}$
• Unit Conversions: $1\text{ bar} = 10^5\text{ Pa} = 100\text{ kPa}$  |  $1\text{ MPa} = 1000\text{ kPa}$
Question 1: Definition, Importance & Applications
1. Define thermodynamics and write its importance and applications.
Step 1: Simple & Clear Definition
• Origin: Derived from Greek words therme (heat) and dynamis (power).
• Definition: Thermodynamics is the science that deals with heat and work, and the transformations of energy from one form to another, along with the properties of the working substance involved.
Step 2: Importance in Engineering
• Helps engineers calculate the efficiency of engines, power plants, and refrigerators.
• Sets the fundamental laws of energy conservation (First Law) and the direction of heat flow (Second Law).
• Guides the design of practical systems without wasting energy.
Step 3: Core Application Areas (Lecture Slide 7)
• Power Generation: Thermal power plants, gas turbines, nuclear plants, wind turbines.
• Transportation: Car engines (internal combustion), airplanes, jet engines, and rockets.
• Heating & Cooling (HVAC): Household refrigerators, air conditioning (AC), and domestic water heaters.
• Human Body: Heart pumping blood, body cells converting food energy, and heat loss to the room.
Question 2: Thermodynamic System & Classifications
2. Define a thermodynamic system. Classify them.
Step 1: Definitions (Lecture Slide 11)
• Thermodynamic System: A quantity of matter or a region in space chosen for study.
• Surroundings: Everything outside the system.
• Boundary: The real or imaginary surface separating the system from its surroundings (can be fixed or moving).
Step 2: Classification by Mass & Energy Flow
1. Closed System (Control Mass): Mass cannot cross the boundary, but energy (heat and work) can cross.
2. Open System (Control Volume): Both mass and energy cross the boundary.
3. Isolated System: Neither mass nor energy can cross the boundary.
Step 3: Classification by Number of Phases (Lecture Slide 17)
1. Homogeneous System: Consists of only a single uniform phase throughout.
2. Heterogeneous System: Consists of two or more distinct phases separated by boundary lines.

Question 4: Homogeneous vs. Heterogeneous Systems
4. How does a homogeneous system differ from a heterogeneous system?
Step 1: Definition of Phase (Lecture Slide 17)
A Phase is a quantity of matter that is completely uniform throughout in chemical composition and physical structure (solid, liquid, or gas).
Step 2: Comparison & Examples
• Homogeneous System: Consists of only a single phase throughout (e.g., Dry air, water mixed with nitric acid).
• Heterogeneous System: Consists of two or more distinct phases separated by visible interfaces (e.g., Water plus steam, ice plus water, water plus oil).
Question 5: Pure Substance Definition
5. What do you mean by a pure substance?
Step 1: Clear Lecture Definition (Lecture Slide 17)
A pure substance is one that has a homogeneous and invariable (fixed) chemical composition throughout all its parts, even if it undergoes a change of phase.
Step 2: Lecture Examples
• Pure Substances: Liquid water, steam, ice, mixture of ice and liquid water, mixture of liquid water and steam, gaseous nitrogen ($N_2$).
• Not a Pure Substance: A mixture of liquid air and gaseous air (because oxygen and nitrogen condense at different temperatures, making the composition of the two phases different).
Question 6: State, Process, Cycle, and Path
6. Explain the following terms: (i) State, (ii) Process, (iii) Cycle, and (iv) Path.
P-V Diagram: State, Process, Path, and Cycle (Lecture Slides 29 & 30)
(i) Thermodynamic State
The condition of a system at any given moment, described completely by its properties ($P, T, V$).
(ii) Thermodynamic Process
Any change that takes a system from one equilibrium state to another.
(iii) Process Path
The continuous series (curve) of states through which a system passes as it moves between initial and final states.
(iv) Thermodynamic Cycle
A sequence of processes where the final state is identical to the initial state ($\Delta \text{Property} = 0$, and $\sum Q = \sum W$).
Question 7: Zeroth Law of Thermodynamics
7. Explain briefly zeroth law of thermodynamics.
Zeroth Law of Thermodynamics (Lecture Slide 24)
Statement of the Law
"If two bodies (A and B) are each in thermal equilibrium with a third body (C), then they are also in thermal equilibrium with each other."
$$\text{If } T_A = T_C \quad \text{and} \quad T_B = T_C \implies T_A = T_B$$
• Serves as the basis of temperature measurement where body C is the thermometer.
Question 8: Thermodynamic Properties
8. Define thermodynamic property. State and differentiate between extensive, intensive and specific properties.
Step 1: Comparison & Partition Rule (Slide 18)
• Intensive: Independent of system mass ($T, P, ho$).
• Extensive: Depends on system mass and size ($m, V, E$).
• Specific: Extensive property divided by mass (becomes intensive, e.g., $v = V/m, h = H/m$).
Question 9: Thermodynamic vs. Thermal Equilibrium
9. What do you mean by thermodynamic equilibrium? How does it differ from thermal equilibrium?
Step 1: Four Conditions (Slide 30)
1. Thermal (uniform $T$), 2. Mechanical (uniform $P$), 3. Chemical (no reactions), 4. Phase (mass of each phase is constant).
• Thermal equilibrium is just one requirement of thermodynamic equilibrium.

📌 Key Formulas & Conversions Summary (Chapter 2: Ideal & Real Gases)
• Ideal Gas Law: $PV = mRT$  |  $Pv = RT$  |  $P = \rho RT$
• Gas Constant: $R = \frac{R_u}{M}$  (Universal $R_u = 8.314\text{ kJ/kmol}\cdot\text{K}$)
• Specific Heats: $C_p - C_v = R$, $\gamma = \frac{C_p}{C_v}$, $C_v = \frac{R}{\gamma - 1}$, $C_p = \frac{\gamma R}{\gamma - 1}$
• Energy Changes: $\Delta U = m C_v \Delta T$  |  $\Delta H = m C_p \Delta T$  |  $\Delta H = \Delta U + \Delta(PV)$
• State Change: $\frac{P_1 V_1}{T_1} = \frac{P_2 V_2}{T_2} = \text{const}$ (Use absolute $T$ in K, $P$ in kPa)
• Real Gas & Compressibility: $PV = ZmRT$, $P_r = \frac{P}{P_c}$, $T_r = \frac{T}{T_c}$
• Conversions: $T(\text{K}) = T(^\circ\text{C}) + 273$, $1\text{ bar} = 100\text{ kPa}$, $1\text{ MPa} = 1000\text{ kPa}$, $1\text{ m}^3 = 1000\text{ L}$, $1\text{ cm}^2 = 10^{-4}\text{ m}^2$
Question 1: Vertical Piston-Cylinder Pressures
1. A vertical placed cylinder piston assembly contains a gas. The total mass of the piston and the additional load placed on it is 20 kg. If the atmospheric pressure is 0.1 MPa and the cross-sectional area of the piston is 10 $cm^2$, calculate the absolute and gauge pressures of the gas.
⚙️ Process Type: Static Force Equilibrium (Vertical Piston)

Given Data: $m = 20\text{ kg}$  |  $A = 10\text{ cm}^2 = 10 \times 10^{-4}\text{ m}^2$  |  $P_{\text{atm}} = 0.1\text{ MPa} = 100\text{ kPa}$  |  $g = 9.81\text{ m/s}^2$
Step 1: Force Balance on Piston (Lecture 2, Slide 51)
• Downward forces: Atmospheric force ($P_{atm} A$) + Piston & Load weight ($W = mg$).
• Upward force: Gas pressure force ($P A$).
$$P \cdot A = P_{atm} \cdot A + m \cdot g \implies P_{abs} = P_{atm} + \frac{m \cdot g}{A}$$
Step 2: Gauge Pressure Calculation
Given: $m = 20\text{ kg}$, $A = 10\text{ cm}^2 = 10 \times 10^{-4}\text{ m}^2$, $g = 9.81\text{ m/s}^2$
$$P_{gage} = \frac{m \cdot g}{A} = \frac{20 \times 9.81}{10 \times 10^{-4}} = 196,200\text{ Pa} = 196.2\text{ kPa}$$
$P_{\text{gage}} = 196.2\text{ kPa (1.962 bar)}$
Step 3: Absolute Pressure Calculation
Given: $P_{atm} = 0.1\text{ MPa} = 100\text{ kPa}$
$$P_{abs} = P_{gage} + P_{atm} = 196.2 + 100 = 296.2\text{ kPa} = 0.2962\text{ MPa}$$
$P_{\text{abs}} = 296.2\text{ kPa} = 0.2962\text{ MPa}$
Question 2: Rigid Tank with Oxygen (O₂)
2. A rigid tank contains 0.5 kg of Oxygen ($O_2$) initially at 4 MPa and 180 K. The gas is cooled, and the pressure drops to 3.3 MPa. Determine the volume of the tank and the final temperature.
🔒 Process Type: Isochoric Process (Constant Volume, V = const)

Given Data: $m = 0.5\text{ kg (O}_2\text{)}$  |  $P_1 = 4\text{ MPa} = 4000\text{ kPa}$  |  $T_1 = 180\text{ K}$  |  $P_2 = 3.3\text{ MPa} = 3300\text{ kPa}$  |  $V_1 = V_2 = \text{const (Rigid)}$
Step 1: Properties of Oxygen & Tank Volume (Lecture 2, Slide 60)
• For Oxygen ($O_2$): $M = 32\text{ kg/kmol} \implies R = \frac{R_u}{M} = \frac{8.314}{32} = 0.25983\text{ kJ/kg}\text{ K}$
• Initial State 1: $m = 0.5\text{ kg}$, $P₁ = 4\text{ MPa} = 4000\text{ kPa}$, $T₁ = 180\text{ K}$
$$P₁ V = m R T₁ \implies V = \frac{m R T₁}{P₁} = \frac{0.5 \times 0.25983 \times 180}{4000} = 0.00585\text{ m}^3$$
$V_{\text{tank}} = 0.00585\text{ m}^3\text{ (5.85 L)}$
Step 2: Final Temperature after Cooling (Constant Volume)
Because the vessel is rigid, $V₁ = V₂ = \text{constant}$. Using the ideal gas law:
$$\frac{P₁}{T₁} = \frac{P₂}{T₂} \implies T₂ = T₁ \left(\frac{P₂}{P₁} \right) = 180 \times \left(\frac{3.3}{4.0} \right) = 148.5\text{ K}$$
$T_{\text{final}} = 148.5\text{ K (-124.65}^\circ\text{C)}$
Question 3: Gas Constant and Specific Heats of CO₂
3. The relative molecular mass of carbon dioxide, $CO_2$ is 44. In an experiment the value of $\gamma$ for $CO_2$ was found to be 1.3. Assuming that $CO_2$ is a perfect gas, calculate the specific gas constant, R, and the specific heat at constant pressure and constant volume, $Cp$ and $Cv$.
🧪 Property Evaluation: Perfect Gas Relations (Cp, Cv, γ, R)

Given Data: $\text{Gas: CO}_2$  |  $M = 44\text{ kg/kmol}$  |  $\gamma = 1.3$  |  $R_u = 8.314\text{ kJ/kmol}\cdot\text{K}$
Step 1: Specific Gas Constant R (Lecture 2, Slide 64)
Universal gas constant $R_u = 8.314\text{ kJ/kmol}\text{ K}$, $M = 44\text{ kg/kmol}$:
$$R = \frac{R_u}{M} = \frac{8.314}{44} = 0.18895\text{ kJ/kg}\text{ K} \approx 0.189\text{ kJ/kg}\text{ K}$$
$R = 0.189\text{ kJ/kg}\cdot\text{K (188.95 J/kg}\cdot\text{K)}$
Step 2: Specific Heat at Constant Volume Cv
Given $\gamma = 1.3$:
$$Cv = \frac{R}{\gamma - 1} = \frac{0.18895}{1.3 - 1} = \frac{0.18895}{0.3} = 0.6298\text{ kJ/kg}\text{ K} \approx 0.63\text{ kJ/kg}\text{ K}$$
$C_v = 0.63\text{ kJ/kg}\cdot\text{K}$
Step 3: Specific Heat at Constant Pressure Cp
$$Cp = \gamma \cdot Cv = 1.3 \times 0.6298 = 0.8188\text{ kJ/kg}\text{ K} \approx 0.819\text{ kJ/kg}\text{ K}$$
Verification: $Cp - Cv = 0.8188 - 0.6298 = 0.189 = R$ ✓
$C_p = 0.819\text{ kJ/kg}\cdot\text{K}$
Question 4: Perfect Gas Expansion
4. A certain perfect gas of mass 0.01 kg occupies a volume of 0.003 $m^3$ at a pressure of 7 bars and a temperature of $131^{\circ}C$. The gas is allowed to expand until the pressure is 1 bar and the final volume is 0.02 $m^3$. Calculate (a) the gas constant and the molecular weight, (b) The final temperature.
P-V Diagram for Gas Expansion Process (Q4)
📈 Process Type: Gas Expansion Process (P₁, V₁ → P₂, V₂)

Given Data: $m = 0.01\text{ kg}$  |  $V_1 = 0.003\text{ m}^3$  |  $P_1 = 7\text{ bar} = 700\text{ kPa}$  |  $T_1 = 131^\circ\text{C} = 404\text{ K}$  |  $P_2 = 1\text{ bar} = 100\text{ kPa}$  |  $V_2 = 0.02\text{ m}^3$
Step 1: Gas Constant R & Molecular Weight M (Lecture 2, Slide 61)
Initial state: $m = 0.01\text{ kg}$, $V₁ = 0.003\text{ m}^3$, $P₁ = 7\text{ bar} = 700\text{ kPa}$, $T₁ = 131 + 273 = 404\text{ K}$
$$P₁ V₁ = m R T₁ \implies R = \frac{P₁ V₁}{m T₁} = \frac{700 \times 0.003}{0.01 \times 404} = \frac{2.1}{4.04} = 0.5198\text{ kJ/kg}\text{ K} \approx 0.52\text{ kJ/kg}\text{ K}$$
$$M = \frac{R_u}{R} = \frac{8.314}{0.5198} = 15.99\text{ kg/kmol} \approx 16\text{ kg/kmol}$$
$R = 0.52\text{ kJ/kg}\cdot\text{K} \quad\mid\quad M = 16\text{ kg/kmol (CH}_4\text{)}$
Step 2: Final Temperature T₂
Final state: $P₂ = 1\text{ bar} = 100\text{ kPa}$, $V₂ = 0.02\text{ m}^3$
$$\frac{P₁ V₁}{T₁} = \frac{P₂ V₂}{T₂} \implies T₂ = T₁ \cdot \left(\frac{P₂ V₂}{P₁ V₁} \right) = 404 \times \frac{100 \times 0.02}{700 \times 0.003} = 404 \times \frac{2}{2.1} = 384.76\text{ K}$$
In Celsius: $T₂ = 384.76 - 273 = 111.76^{\circ}\text{C}$
$T_2 = 384.76\text{ K} = 111.76^\circ\text{C}$
Question 5: Real vs. Ideal Gas Model for CO₂ (Compressibility Chart)
5. A 0.3 $m^3$ tank contains carbon dioxide gas at $37^{\circ}C$, 2.2 MPa. (a) Determine the mass inside the tank, using the compressibility factor chart. (b) Estimate the percent error in estimating the mass inside the tank if the ideal gas model is assumed.
🔬 Model Type: Real Gas Evaluation (Generalized Compressibility Z-Chart)

Given Data: $\text{Gas: CO}_2$  |  $V = 0.3\text{ m}^3$  |  $T = 37^\circ\text{C} = 310\text{ K}$  |  $P = 2.2\text{ MPa} = 2200\text{ kPa}$  |  $P_c = 7.39\text{ MPa}$, $T_c = 304.2\text{ K}$, $R = 0.1889\text{ kJ/kg}\cdot\text{K}$
Step 1: Reduced Coordinates (Lecture 2, Slide 68 & 69)
From Table A-1 for $CO_2$: $P_c = 7.39\text{ MPa}$, $T_c = 304.2\text{ K}$, $R = 0.1889\text{ kJ/kg}\text{ K}$
Given: $P = 2.2\text{ MPa}$, $T = 37 + 273 = 310\text{ K}$, $V = 0.3\text{ m}^3$
$$P_r = \frac{P}{P_c} = \frac{2.2}{7.39} = 0.298 \approx 0.30$$
$$T_r = \frac{T}{T_c} = \frac{310}{304.2} = 1.019 \approx 1.02$$
From Compressibility Chart at $P_r \approx 0.30$ and $T_r \approx 1.02$:  $Z \approx 0.89$
Step 2: Real Mass using Compressibility Factor (Z)
$$P V = Z m_{actual} R T \implies m_{actual} = \frac{P V}{Z R T} = \frac{2200 \times 0.3}{0.89 \times 0.1889 \times 310} = \frac{660}{52.12} = 12.66\text{ kg}$$
$m_{\text{actual}} = 12.66\text{ kg}$
Step 3: Ideal Gas Mass & Percent Error
Ideal gas model ($Z = 1$):
$$m_{ideal} = \frac{P V}{R T} = \frac{2200 \times 0.3}{0.1889 \times 310} = 11.27\text{ kg}$$
$$\text{Percent Error} = \frac{|m_{actual} - m_{ideal}|}{m_{actual}} \times 100\% = \frac{12.66 - 11.27}{12.66} \times 100\% \approx 11.0\%$$
$\text{Error} = 11.0\%\text{ (Underestimation by ideal gas model)}$
Question 6: Automobile Tire Pressure & Temperature
6. The gage pressure of an automobile tire is measured to be 210 kPa before a trip and 220 kPa after the trip at a location where the atmospheric pressure is 95 kPa. Assuming the volume of the tire remains constant and the air temperature before the trip is $25^{\circ}C$, determine air temperature in the tire after the trip.
🚗 Process Type: Isochoric Process (Rigid Tire, V = const)

Given Data: $P_{\text{gage},1} = 210\text{ kPa}$  |  $P_{\text{gage},2} = 220\text{ kPa}$  |  $P_{\text{atm}} = 95\text{ kPa}$  |  $T_1 = 25^\circ\text{C} = 298\text{ K}$  |  $V = \text{const}$
Step 1: Absolute Pressures (Lecture 2, Slide 58)
Always use absolute pressure in ideal gas equations:
$$P₁ = P_{gage,1} + P_{atm} = 210 + 95 = 305\text{ kPa}$$
$$P₂ = P_{gage,2} + P_{atm} = 220 + 95 = 315\text{ kPa}$$
Step 2: Temperature Calculation (Isochoric Process)
Given $V₁ = V₂$ and $T₁ = 25 + 273 = 298\text{ K}$:
$$\frac{P₁}{T₁} = \frac{P₂}{T₂} \implies T₂ = T₁ \cdot \left(\frac{P₂}{P₁} \right) = 298 \times \left(\frac{315}{305} \right) = 307.8\text{ K}$$
In Celsius: $T₂ = 307.8 - 273 = 34.8^{\circ}\text{C}$
$T_2 = 307.8\text{ K} = 34.8^\circ\text{C}$
Question 7: Mixing of Gas in Two Connected Tanks
7. Two tanks are connected by a valve. One tank contains 2 kg of Carbon monoxide gas at $77^{\circ}C$ and 0.7 bar. The other tank holds 8 kg of the same gas at $27^{\circ}C$ and 1.2 bar. The valve is opened, and the gases are allowed to mix until reaching to an equilibrium state. The equilibrium temperature is $42^{\circ}C$. Using the ideal gas model, determine the final equilibrium pressure, in bars.
🔄 Process Type: Adiabatic Mixing & Equilibrium (Two Connected Tanks)

Given Data: $\text{Tank A: } m_A = 2\text{ kg}, T_A = 77^\circ\text{C} = 350\text{ K}, P_A = 0.7\text{ bar} = 70\text{ kPa}$
$\text{Tank B: } m_B = 8\text{ kg}, T_B = 27^\circ\text{C} = 300\text{ K}, P_B = 1.2\text{ bar} = 120\text{ kPa}$  |  $T_{\text{final}} = 42^\circ\text{C} = 315\text{ K}$  |  $R_{\text{CO}} = 0.2968\text{ kJ/kg}\cdot\text{K}$
Step 1: Initial Volumes of Tanks A & B (Lecture 2, Slide 58)
For Carbon Monoxide ($CO$): $R = 0.2968\text{ kJ/kg}\text{ K}$
• Tank A: $m_A = 2\text{ kg}$, $T_A = 77 + 273 = 350\text{ K}$, $P_A = 0.7\text{ bar} = 70\text{ kPa}$
$$V_A = \frac{m_A R T_A}{P_A} = \frac{2 \times 0.2968 \times 350}{70} = 2.968\text{ m}^3$$
• Tank B: $m_B = 8\text{ kg}$, $T_B = 27 + 273 = 300\text{ K}$, $P_B = 1.2\text{ bar} = 120\text{ kPa}$
$$V_B = \frac{m_B R T_B}{P_B} = \frac{8 \times 0.2968 \times 300}{120} = 5.936\text{ m}^3$$
Total volume: $V_{total} = V_A + V_B = 2.968 + 5.936 = 8.904\text{ m}^3$
Step 2: Final Equilibrium Pressure
Total mass: $m_{total} = 2 + 8 = 10\text{ kg}$, $T_{final} = 42 + 273 = 315\text{ K}$
$$P_{final} = \frac{m_{total} R T_{final}}{V_{total}} = \frac{10 \times 0.2968 \times 315}{8.904} = \frac{934.92}{8.904} = 105\text{ kPa} = 1.05\text{ bar}$$
$P_{\text{final}} = 105\text{ kPa} = 1.05\text{ bar}$
Question 8: Engine Compression Stroke
8. The compression ratio of an engine is 15 to 1. The pressure of the gas at the beginning of the stroke is 100 kPa and the temperature is $30^{\circ}C$. Calculate the absolute pressure at the end of compression stroke, if the temperature used to be $980^{\circ}C$.
⚡ Process Type: Engine Compression Stroke (Ratio 15 : 1)

Given Data: $\text{Compression ratio } r = V_1 / V_2 = 15$  |  $P_1 = 100\text{ kPa}$  |  $T_1 = 30^\circ\text{C} = 303\text{ K}$  |  $T_2 = 980^\circ\text{C} = 1253\text{ K}$
Step 1: Compression Ratio & State Relations (Lecture 2, Slide 59)
• Compression ratio: $r = \frac{V₁}{V₂} = 15 \implies V₁ = 15 V₂$
• State 1: $P₁ = 100\text{ kPa}$, $T₁ = 30 + 273 = 303\text{ K}$
• State 2: $T₂ = 980 + 273 = 1253\text{ K}$
Step 2: Final Absolute Pressure Calculation
$$\frac{P₁ V₁}{T₁} = \frac{P₂ V₂}{T₂} \implies P₂ = P₁ \cdot \left(\frac{V₁}{V₂} \right) \cdot \left(\frac{T₂}{T₁} \right)$$
$$P₂ = 100 \times 15 \times \left(\frac{1253}{303} \right) = 1500 \times 4.1353 = 6203\text{ kPa} = 62.03\text{ bar}$$
$P_2 = 6203\text{ kPa} = 62.03\text{ bar} = 6.203\text{ MPa}$
Question 9: Additional Mass Added to Rigid Tank
9. 10 kg of carbon monoxide at $40^{\circ}C$ occupies 3 $m^3$. Determine the gas pressure in bar. An additional mass of carbon monoxide is then very slowly added to raise the tank pressure to 10 bar. Assuming that the gas temperature remains constant, how much extra mass has been added? Assume $R = 0.297\text{ kJ/kg}\text{ K}$.
🌡️ Process Type: Isothermal Process (Constant Temp, T = const)

Given Data: $m_1 = 10\text{ kg (CO)}$  |  $T = 40^\circ\text{C} = 313\text{ K (const)}$  |  $V = 3\text{ m}^3\text{ (const)}$  |  $P_2 = 10\text{ bar} = 1000\text{ kPa}$  |  $R = 0.297\text{ kJ/kg}\cdot\text{K}$
Step 1: Initial Pressure P₁
Given: $m_1 = 10\text{ kg}$, $V = 3\text{ m}^3$, $T = 40 + 273 = 313\text{ K}$, $R = 0.297\text{ kJ/kg}\text{ K}$
$$P₁ V = m_1 R T \implies P₁ = \frac{m_1 R T}{V} = \frac{10 \times 0.297 \times 313}{3} = \frac{929.61}{3} = 309.87\text{ kPa} = 3.099\text{ bar}$$
$P_1 = 309.87\text{ kPa} = 3.099\text{ bar}$
Step 2: Total Final Mass m_2 at 10 bar
Final state: $P₂ = 10\text{ bar} = 1000\text{ kPa}$, $T = 313\text{ K}$, $V = 3\text{ m}^3$
$$m_2 = \frac{P₂ V}{R T} = \frac{1000 \times 3}{0.297 \times 313} = \frac{3000}{92.961} = 32.27\text{ kg}$$
Step 3: Extra Mass Added
$$\Delta m = m_2 - m_1 = 32.27 - 10 = 22.27\text{ kg}$$
$\Delta m = 22.27\text{ kg (Extra Mass Added)}$
Question 10: Heating Air in Rigid Vessel (Internal Energy & Enthalpy)
10. 10 kg of air is heated in a rigid vessel from $20^{\circ}C$ to $100^{\circ}C$. If the ratio of specific heat is 1.4, estimate the values of Cp and Cv, change in internal energy, and enthalpy.
🔥 Process Type: Isochoric Heating (Rigid Vessel, V = const)

Given Data: $m = 10\text{ kg (Air)}$  |  $T_1 = 20^\circ\text{C} = 293\text{ K}$  |  $T_2 = 100^\circ\text{C} = 373\text{ K}$  |  $\gamma = 1.4$  |  $R_{\text{air}} = 0.287\text{ kJ/kg}\cdot\text{K}$
Step 1: Specific Heats of Air Cv and Cp (Lecture 2, Slide 65)
For air: $R = 0.287\text{ kJ/kg}\text{ K}$, $\gamma = 1.4$
$$Cv = \frac{R}{\gamma - 1} = \frac{0.287}{1.4 - 1} = 0.7175\text{ kJ/kg}\text{ K}$$
$$Cp = Cv + R = 0.7175 + 0.287 = 1.0045\text{ kJ/kg}\text{ K}$$
$C_v = 0.7175\text{ kJ/kg}\cdot\text{K} \quad\mid\quad C_p = 1.0045\text{ kJ/kg}\cdot\text{K}$
Step 2: Change in Internal Energy (ΔU)
Given: $m = 10\text{ kg}$, $\Delta T = 100 - 20 = 80\text{ K}$
$$\Delta U = m \cdot Cv \cdot (T₂ - T₁) = 10 \times 0.7175 \times 80 = 574\text{ kJ}$$
$\Delta U = 574\text{ kJ}$
Step 3: Change in Enthalpy (ΔH)
$$\Delta H = m \cdot Cp \cdot (T₂ - T₁) = 10 \times 1.0045 \times 80 = 803.6\text{ kJ}$$
$\Delta H = 803.6\text{ kJ}$
Question 11: Enthalpy Decrease & Gas Properties Determination
11. A gas initially at a pressure of 510 kPa and a volume of 142 litres undergoes a process and has a final pressure of 170 kPa and a volume of 275 litres. During the process, the enthalpy decreases by 65 kJ. Take $Cv = 0.718\text{ kJ/kg}\text{ K}$. Determine (a) change in internal energy, (b) specific heat at constant pressure, and (c) specific gas constant.
📉 Process Type: General Non-Flow Process (ΔH & ΔU Analysis)

Given Data: $P_1 = 510\text{ kPa}, V_1 = 142\text{ L} = 0.142\text{ m}^3$  |  $P_2 = 170\text{ kPa}, V_2 = 275\text{ L} = 0.275\text{ m}^3$
$\Delta H = -65\text{ kJ}$  |  $Cv = 0.718\text{ kJ/kg}\cdot\text{K}$
Step 1: Change in Internal Energy (ΔU) (Lecture 2, Slide 66)
Given: $P₁ = 510\text{ kPa}$, $V₁ = 142\text{ L} = 0.142\text{ m}^3$
$P₂ = 170\text{ kPa}$, $V₂ = 275\text{ L} = 0.275\text{ m}^3$, $\Delta H = -65\text{ kJ}$
By definition: $H = U + P V \implies \Delta H = \Delta U + (P₂ V₂ - P₁ V₁)$
$$P₂ V₂ - P₁ V₁ = (170 \times 0.275) - (510 \times 0.142) = 46.75 - 72.42 = -25.67\text{ kJ}$$
$$\Delta U = \Delta H - (P₂ V₂ - P₁ V₁) = -65 - (-25.67) = -39.33\text{ kJ}$$
$\Delta U = -39.33\text{ kJ (Decrease of 39.33 kJ)}$
Step 2: Specific Heat at Constant Pressure (Cp)
For an ideal gas: $\Delta U = m Cv \Delta T$ and $\Delta H = m Cp \Delta T$
$$\frac{\Delta H}{\Delta U} = \frac{m Cp \Delta T}{m Cv \Delta T} = \frac{Cp}{Cv}$$
$$Cp = Cv \cdot \left(\frac{\Delta H}{\Delta U} \right) = 0.718 \times \left(\frac{-65}{-39.33} \right) = 0.718 \times 1.6527 = 1.1866\text{ kJ/kg}\text{ K}$$
$C_p = 1.187\text{ kJ/kg}\cdot\text{K}$
Step 3: Specific Gas Constant (R)
$$R = Cp - Cv = 1.1866 - 0.718 = 0.4686\text{ kJ/kg}\text{ K} \approx 0.469\text{ kJ/kg}\text{ K}$$
$R = 0.469\text{ kJ/kg}\cdot\text{K (468.6 J/kg}\cdot\text{K)}$
